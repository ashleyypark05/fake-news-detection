Fake News Detection

Comparing Classical and Transformer-Based Approaches to Misinformation Classification

This project benchmarks four text representation strategies for binary fake news classification on the WELFake dataset (~72,000 labeled news articles). Classical approaches — TF-IDF, Word2Vec, Doc2Vec, and Sentence2Vec — are evaluated as logistic regression baselines before a fine-tuned BERT model is trained as the upper-bound benchmark. TF-IDF achieves the strongest baseline at 94.2% accuracy, outperforming all embedding-based approaches under logistic regression. BERT fine-tuning is expected to push above 98%, at the cost of substantially greater compute. The project frames this as a practical trade-off analysis: when does the complexity of a transformer model justify the accuracy gain over a well-tuned classical baseline?

1. Introduction & Research Questions

The spread of misinformation through online news platforms has become a measurable social problem. Automated detection systems can serve as one layer of a content moderation pipeline — not as a sole arbiter, but as a scalable first-pass filter. The central challenge is choosing the right representation: does the semantic richness of a transformer model meaningfully outperform simpler frequency-based features, or does TF-IDF capture enough surface-level signal to be sufficient for practical deployment?

"Across classical and transformer-based text representations, which approach best balances classification accuracy and computational cost for large-scale fake news detection?"

  • Baseline comparison — How do TF-IDF, Word2Vec, Doc2Vec, and Sentence2Vec compare under a consistent logistic regression classifier?
  • Transformer ceiling — What accuracy gain does BERT fine-tuning provide over the best classical baseline?
  • Practical trade-off — Is the compute cost of BERT justifiable given the marginal accuracy improvement over TF-IDF?

2. Dataset & Preprocessing

The WELFake dataset combines four existing fake news datasets (Kaggle, McIntire, Reuters, BuzzFeed Political) into a single labeled corpus. It contains approximately 72,134 articles split roughly evenly between real and fake labels, making class imbalance a non-issue for baseline modeling.

3. Feature Engineering

Four distinct text representation strategies were implemented and evaluated. Each represents a different assumption about what makes text meaningful for classification.

TF-IDF

Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency produces a sparse, high-dimensional feature matrix weighted by how distinctive a term is across the corpus. No semantic relationships are encoded — words are treated as independent signals. Implemented with sklearn.TfidfVectorizer with unigram and bigram features.

Word2Vec

A shallow neural network trained on the corpus to produce dense 100-dimensional word embeddings. Document-level representations are produced by averaging the word vectors for each article. Captures local semantic relationships (synonyms, analogies) but loses document structure in the averaging step.

Doc2Vec

An extension of Word2Vec that learns document-level embeddings directly, preserving more structural context than averaged Word2Vec. Trained using the PV-DM architecture with 100-dimensional vectors over 40 epochs.

Sentence2Vec (Sentence Transformers)

Pre-trained sentence transformer embeddings (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) producing 384-dimensional dense vectors. Unlike the other embedding approaches, Sentence2Vec is not trained on the WELFake corpus — it uses transfer learning from a large general-purpose model. This makes it the most semantically rich of the classical baselines but also the most computationally expensive to encode.

All four representations are implemented in src/vectorizers.py with a consistent interface.

4. Baseline Model Results

All four feature representations were evaluated under logistic regression (L2 regularization, max_iter=1000) on the same 80/20 stratified split. Accuracy is the primary metric given balanced classes; F1 scores are consistent with accuracy results.

TF-IDF Dominates Classical Baselines TF-IDF outperforms all three embedding-based approaches under logistic regression by 3–16 percentage points. This is consistent with the broader NLP literature: for binary classification tasks with sufficient labeled data, lexical features often dominate over semantic embeddings when the classifier is simple. Fake news articles tend to have distinctive surface-level vocabulary patterns that TF-IDF captures effectively.
Sentence2Vec Underperformance The Sentence2Vec result (78.0%) is notably lower than expected for a pre-trained transformer embedding. The likely cause is domain mismatch — all-MiniLM-L6-v2 was trained on general-purpose sentence similarity tasks, not news classification. Fine-tuning a sentence transformer on this corpus would likely close the gap substantially.

5. BERT Fine-Tuning

The final stage fine-tunes bert-base-uncased on the WELFake training set with a binary classification head. This serves as the performance ceiling benchmark — the question being whether the ~3 percentage point gain over TF-IDF justifies the orders-of-magnitude increase in compute and model complexity.

6. Discussion

The TF-IDF Ceiling Problem

The strongest result in this study — TF-IDF at 94.2% — raises an important practical question: at what point does additional model complexity stop being justified? For a production content moderation system processing millions of articles daily, TF-IDF with logistic regression is orders of magnitude faster to train, simpler to deploy, and easier to inspect than a fine-tuned BERT model.

The ~3 percentage point gap between TF-IDF and expected BERT performance translates to roughly 2,000 additional correctly classified articles per 72,000. Whether this matters depends entirely on the downstream cost of misclassification — a high-stakes moderation system would value the gain; a low-stakes research filter might not.

Why Embeddings Underperform Here

The embedding-based approaches (Word2Vec, Doc2Vec, Sentence2Vec) all underperform TF-IDF under logistic regression for a predictable reason: fake news detection at the article level is heavily lexical. Fake articles tend to use specific vocabulary patterns — emotionally charged language, specific source citations that real news doesn't use, particular syntactic constructions. TF-IDF captures exactly these surface signals. Semantic embeddings smooth over precisely the features that are most discriminative.

Practical Recommendation For production deployment, a TF-IDF + logistic regression pipeline offers the best balance of accuracy, speed, and interpretability. BERT fine-tuning is the right choice when accuracy above 94% is a hard requirement, compute is available, and explainability is less critical. The two approaches are complementary, not competitive — BERT can serve as a second-pass filter on articles that TF-IDF classifies with low confidence.

7. Limitations & Future Work

Dataset composition. WELFake merges four datasets with different collection methodologies and time periods. Models trained on this corpus may not generalize to news from different domains, languages, or platforms.

Binary framing. Real-world misinformation exists on a spectrum — partially false, misleading framing, out-of-context imagery. A binary real/fake label collapses this nuance and may incentivize models to pick up on surface artifacts of the labeling process rather than genuine misinformation signals.

Doc2Vec underperformance. The Doc2Vec result (89.7%) may improve with longer training (more epochs, larger vector size). The current configuration was chosen for speed; a full hyperparameter sweep could close the gap with Word2Vec.

Future work. Natural extensions include: (1) replacing Sentence2Vec with a news-domain fine-tuned sentence transformer; (2) adding metadata features (publication source, publication date, article length) to the classical pipelines; (3) evaluating on a held-out temporal split to test generalization over time; (4) exploring RoBERTa or DeBERTa as alternatives to BERT-base.
